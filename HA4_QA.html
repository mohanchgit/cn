01 Handoff in cellular networks: hard and soft handoff

What is a handoff?

A handoff (handover) is the process of transferring an active mobile call or data connection from one cell/base station to another as a user moves, or when radio conditions change. The aim is to keep the connection working with little or no interruption.

Mobile in Cell A→Handoff decision→Cell B

Hard handoff – break before make

The mobile disconnects from the old base station before establishing the new radio link. At a particular instant, it is connected to only one base station. It is common in GSM and many LTE handover procedures.

Example: A person travelling by car leaves Cell A. The phone releases its connection to Cell A and then connects to Cell B. A very short interruption may occur.

Soft handoff – make before break

The mobile establishes a connection with the new base station before releasing the old one. For a short period, it can communicate with more than one base station. This is associated particularly with CDMA systems.

Example: A CDMA user near the boundary of two cells is served by both base stations during the transition; the weaker/old link is removed after the new link is reliable.

FeatureHard handoffSoft handoff
MethodBreak before makeMake before break
Simultaneous linksNormally oneMore than one temporarily
InterruptionPossible brief breakUsually smoother
ComplexitySimplerMore coordination/resources
Typical exampleGSMCDMA
Remember: Hard = disconnect then connect. Soft = connect the new link, then disconnect the old link.
02 MIME: RFC 822-style headers and media types

Why MIME?

Traditional Internet email was designed mainly for ASCII text. MIME (Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions) extends email so it can carry attachments, images, audio, video and non-ASCII text.

Illustrative email headers

From: sender@example.com
To: learner@example.com
Date: Fri, 13 Nov 2026 10:00:00 +0530
Subject: Network notes
MIME-Version: 1.0
Content-Type: multipart/mixed; boundary="PART"

--PART
Content-Type: text/plain; charset="UTF-8"
Content-Transfer-Encoding: 8bit

Please find the notes attached.

--PART
Content-Type: application/pdf
Content-Transfer-Encoding: base64
Content-Disposition: attachment; filename="notes.pdf"

[encoded attachment data]
--PART--

The first four lines are common message headers from the RFC 822 message format (now updated by RFC 5322). MIME adds MIME-specific headers such as MIME-Version, Content-Type and Content-Transfer-Encoding.

Important MIME headers

HeaderPurpose
MIME-VersionIndicates the MIME version, commonly 1.0.
Content-TypeSpecifies the media type and optional parameters such as charset or boundary.
Content-Transfer-EncodingDescribes encoding used for safe email transport, e.g. 7bit, 8bit, binary, quoted-printable, base64.
Content-IDProvides an identifier for a body part, useful for inline content.
Content-DescriptionHuman-readable description of the body part.
Content-DispositionIndicates inline display or attachment; commonly includes a filename.

Common MIME media types and subtypes

A media type has the form type/subtype. The following are common examples; MIME's registered subtype list is much larger and continues to evolve.

Top-level typeExample subtypesTypical content
textplain, html, css, csvText, web pages, stylesheets

Exam point: RFC 2045 defines MIME message format, headers and transfer encodings; media-type definitions are specified mainly in RFC 2046. Do not confuse the RFC number with the complete list of all registered subtypes.
03 Advantages and limitations of wireless communication

Wireless communication transfers information through electromagnetic waves instead of a physical cable.

AdvantagesLimitations
Mobility: users can move while connected.Coverage is limited by distance, obstacles and access-point power.
Easy deployment: less cabling is needed.Interference from other devices and networks can reduce quality.
Flexible expansion: devices can be added more easily.Shared radio medium can cause congestion and variable speed.
Useful in difficult locations where cables are costly.Radio signals may be intercepted; strong security is essential.
Supports mobile, temporary and IoT applications.Signal strength can change due to walls, weather and movement.
Can reduce installation and relocation effort.Battery-powered devices need energy for radio transmission.
Example: Wi-Fi lets students use laptops anywhere in a classroom. However, a concrete wall and many simultaneous users may reduce speed. A wired Ethernet link is usually more predictable for a fixed desktop or server.
04 Radio waves, microwaves and infrared waves

All three are electromagnetic waves. Frequency and wavelength are related by c = f × λ, where c is approximately 3 × 108 m/s in vacuum, f is frequency in hertz and λ is wavelength in metres. Higher frequency means shorter wavelength.

Wave band (approximate)FrequencyWavelengthExamples / uses
Radio waves3 kHz–300 MHz (broad conventional range)100 km–1 mAM/FM broadcasting, some navigation and communication systems
Microwaves300 MHz–300 GHz1 m–1 mmWi-Fi (2.4/5/6 GHz), radar, satellite links, cellular systems, microwave links
InfraredAbout 300 GHz–400 THzAbout 1 mm–750 nmTV remote controls, short-range device links, thermal imaging, fibre-optic systems use near-infrared light
Radio: longer wavelength→Microwave: shorter→Infrared: much shorter
Note: Band boundaries are approximate and conventions can overlap. Infrared is often treated separately from radio in communication textbooks, although it is also electromagnetic radiation.

Simple example

  • FM radio can cover a broad area from a broadcast tower.
  • A microwave Wi-Fi signal connects a laptop to a nearby access point.
  • An infrared remote sends a short-range signal to a television, usually requiring a reasonably clear path.
05 GSM architecture and major components

GSM (Global System for Mobile Communications) is a 2G digital cellular system. Its architecture is commonly grouped into the Mobile Station, Base Station Subsystem, Network and Switching Subsystem, and Operations Support Subsystem.

Mobile Station (MS)↔BTS↔BSC↔MSC / GMSC↔PSTN / other networks

HLR · VLR · AuC · EIR support the switching network; OSS monitors and manages the system.

ComponentFunction in simple words
MS (Mobile Station)User's handset plus SIM; accesses the cellular network.
BTS (Base Transceiver Station)Radio equipment that communicates with mobile phones in a cell.
BSC (Base Station Controller)Controls multiple BTSs, radio-channel allocation and many handover functions.
MSC (Mobile Switching Centre)Switches calls, manages mobility and connects to other networks.
GMSC (Gateway MSC)Gateway between the GSM network and external telephone networks.
HLR (Home Location Register)Main subscriber database: service profile and home records.
VLR (Visitor Location Register)Temporary subscriber and location information for an MSC area.
AuC (Authentication Centre)Stores security information and supports SIM authentication.
EIR (Equipment Identity Register)Checks device identity (IMEI), including barred devices.
OSS / OMCOperations, maintenance, monitoring and network management.
Call example: A phone sends a call request to its BTS. The BSC manages radio resources, and the MSC routes the call. Subscriber and authentication databases help verify the user and provide service.
06 How a browser accesses a web page: WWW and HTTP

The World Wide Web (WWW) is a system of linked resources identified by URLs and accessed over the Internet. HTTP is the application-layer protocol used for request-and-response communication between a client and a web server. HTTPS is HTTP protected by TLS.

1. Enter URL→2. DNS lookup→3. Connect→4. HTTP request→5. Response→6. Render page
  1. User enters a URL: e.g. https://www.example.com/index.html. The browser identifies the scheme, host and resource path.
  2. DNS resolution: The browser/OS resolver finds the server's IP address, possibly using cached information.
  3. Connection setup: The browser establishes a transport connection (commonly TCP for HTTP/1.1 or HTTP/2; HTTP/3 uses QUIC over UDP). For HTTPS, TLS security is established.
  4. HTTP request: The browser sends a request such as GET /index.html with headers describing the host and accepted formats.
  5. Server processing: The server or application processes the request and locates or generates the requested resource.
  6. HTTP response: The server returns a status code (e.g. 200 OK, 404 Not Found), response headers and a body such as HTML.
  7. Browser rendering: The browser parses HTML, fetches linked CSS, JavaScript, images and fonts, builds the page layout, runs scripts and displays the page.

Illustrative HTTP exchange

GET /index.html HTTP/1.1
Host: www.example.com
Accept: text/html

HTTP/1.1 200 OK
Content-Type: text/html; charset=UTF-8

<html>...page content...</html>
Remember: DNS finds the server's address; TCP/QUIC provides transport; TLS protects HTTPS; HTTP defines the web request and response; HTML/CSS/JavaScript build the visible page.
07 Ad hoc wireless networks: MANET, VANET, WSN and WRSN

An ad hoc network is formed by wireless nodes without depending on a fixed infrastructure such as a conventional access point or base station for every communication. Nodes may communicate directly or forward packets for other nodes.

Node A↔Node B (relay)↔Node C

If A cannot reach C directly, B can forward the packet.

NetworkArchitecture / exampleFeaturesChallenges
MANET – Mobile Ad hoc NetworkMobile phones/laptops/vehicles form a changing multi-hop network.No fixed infrastructure; self-organising; dynamic routes.Rapid topology changes, routing overhead, security, limited battery and bandwidth.
VANET – Vehicular Ad hoc NetworkVehicles communicate with vehicles (V2V) and roadside units (V2I).High mobility; supports safety alerts and traffic information.Very fast topology changes, reliable low-latency delivery, handover and privacy.
WSN – Wireless Sensor NetworkMany sensor nodes send readings to a sink/base station.Small, low-cost nodes; sensing; often energy-aware and multi-hop.Limited battery, processing and memory; coverage; node failures; security.
WRSN – Wireless Rechargeable Sensor NetworkWSN nodes plus wireless energy-transfer/recharging facilities, often mobile chargers.Extends sensor lifetime and can replenish node batteries.Charging scheduling, energy-transfer efficiency, charger travel time and fairness.

Typical architecture

  • Sensor / mobile nodes: sense data and communicate over wireless links.
  • Routing layer: chooses paths, sometimes through intermediate nodes.
  • Sink / gateway: gathers information and forwards it to an application or Internet.
  • Management / security: handles energy, mobility, authentication and network monitoring.
Example: In a forest WSN, temperature sensors send readings through neighbouring sensors to a gateway. In a WRSN, a mobile charging unit periodically visits low-energy sensors.
08 DNS lookup: recursive, iterative and non-recursive queries

DNS translates a domain name such as www.example.com into records such as an IP address. The three terms describe how much work the queried DNS server is expected to do and whether it already knows the answer.

1. Recursive query

The client asks a DNS resolver to return a final answer or an error. The resolver takes responsibility for finding the answer by querying other DNS servers as needed.

Client→Recursive resolver⇢Root / TLD / authoritative DNS

Final answer returns from resolver to client.

2. Iterative query

The queried DNS server returns the best information it has, often a referral to another server. The requester then contacts that server and continues until it obtains the answer.

Resolver→Root: referral→TLD: referral→Authoritative: answer

3. Non-recursive query

The server responds using information it already has, such as a cached record or authoritative data. It does not go and query other servers to complete the lookup. The response may be the requested record or a referral, depending on the server and query.

Query typeWho does further lookup?Typical response
RecursiveThe queried resolverFinal answer or failure
IterativeThe requester follows referralsBest available answer/referral
Non-recursiveNo additional lookup by that serverCached/authoritative answer or referral
Important distinction: A common real-world sequence is a laptop making a recursive request to its configured resolver, while that resolver makes iterative queries to root, TLD and authoritative servers. A cached answer can make the resolver's response immediate.
09 Wi-Fi versus Bluetooth

Both are short-range wireless technologies, but they are designed for different purposes. Wi-Fi is generally used for network access and higher throughput; Bluetooth is designed for convenient device-to-device connections with low energy options.

FactorWi-FiBluetooth
StandardsIEEE 802.11 familyBluetooth SIG specifications (not IEEE 802.11)
ArchitectureCommonly infrastructure mode: stations connect through an access point; also supports Wi-Fi Direct and mesh variants.Central/peripheral roles in BLE; classic Bluetooth commonly forms piconets, with scatternet support in the specification.
FrequencyCommon bands: 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz, depending on region and device.2.4 GHz ISM band.
RangeOften tens of metres indoors; varies widely with AP, band, walls and power.Often around 10 m for common devices; class and environment can make it shorter or longer.
Data rateFrom tens/hundreds of Mbps to multi-Gbps theoretical PHY rates in modern standards.Lower in general; varies by Bluetooth version and mode. BLE prioritises low-power data exchange.
Power useUsually higher than BLE for continuous high-throughput networking.BLE is designed for very low power; classic Bluetooth power varies by profile and use.
ApplicationsInternet access, video streaming, file transfer, office LANs.Headphones, keyboards, watches, sensors, beacons and peripherals.
Example: A laptop uses Wi-Fi to connect to the university network and Bluetooth to connect to wireless earbuds or a mouse.
10 Design a Wi-Fi LAN for a three-floor organisation with 150 employees

1. Requirements and assumptions

Assume 150 employees are distributed evenly (about 50 per floor), with laptops and phones, cloud applications, video meetings, printers and guest access. The design below is a starting proposal; a physical site survey and capacity test are needed before final AP placement.

2. Suggested logical and physical design

Internet / ISP↔Firewall / Router↔Core PoE Switch

Fibre or high-speed Ethernet uplinks to each floor's access switch

Floor 1 switch → 2–3 APsFloor 2 switch → 2–3 APsFloor 3 switch → 2–3 APs

Employee, guest and IoT devices connect wirelessly to the APs.

  • Access points: Start planning around 2–3 centrally placed Wi-Fi 6/6E APs per floor (6–9 total). This is an initial estimate, not a guaranteed capacity figure. Final quantity depends on floor area, walls, device count, traffic and RF survey.
  • Backhaul: Connect APs to managed PoE switches using Cat6/Cat6A Ethernet; use fibre or high-speed uplinks between floors where appropriate.
  • SSID and security: Provide an employee SSID with WPA2-Enterprise/WPA3-Enterprise and 802.1X authentication; isolate guest Wi-Fi from internal resources; use a separate IoT network where needed.
  • VLANs: Example VLANs: Staff, Guest, IoT/Printers and Network Management. Apply firewall rules and access-control policies between them.
  • Addressing: Use DHCP scopes for each VLAN/subnet; reserve addresses for infrastructure such as printers and management interfaces.
  • Capacity: Estimate concurrent users and devices, not just employee headcount. A person may have a laptop and phone active at the same time.
  • Resilience: Use centrally managed APs, monitor AP health, provide UPS for network switches and consider redundant uplinks/core equipment.

3. Factors affecting Wi-Fi performance

FactorEffectImprovement
Distance and obstaclesWalls, floors and metal weaken signals.Survey and place APs for suitable coverage; avoid hidden AP locations.
InterferenceNeighbouring Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and other RF sources cause contention.Plan channels; use 5/6 GHz where supported; use 2.4 GHz selectively.
Channel width and reuseWide channels can raise peak rate but reduce available non-overlapping channel choices.Choose channel width based on density and interference; coordinate AP channels.
Number of active clientsClients share airtime; busy APs increase delay.Distribute clients across APs; add capacity based on measured demand.
Signal quality (RSSI/SNR)Weak signal and noise cause lower modulation rates and retries.Improve placement, coverage and channel planning.
Backhaul / Internet capacityA fast radio cannot overcome a slow switch uplink or ISP link.Size switch uplinks, PoE and Internet bandwidth for peak demand.
Security and configurationLegacy settings, excessive broadcast traffic or misconfiguration can affect service.Use current security, firmware updates, VLANs and sensible QoS.
Roaming and mobilityPoor cell overlap or sticky clients can interrupt calls.Plan coverage overlap and validate roaming in a walk test.

4. Simple planning calculation

150 employees ÷ 3 floors = approximately 50 employees per floor. If each employee has two devices, plan for about 100 client devices per floor at full participation. With three APs per floor, that averages about 33 devices per AP, but actual performance depends on airtime demand and device capabilities—not simply the client count.

Design caution: Do not select AP count using a fixed “users per AP” rule alone. Conduct a predictive design and on-site RF survey, test peak-hour throughput/latency, and adjust AP placement, channel plan and capacity. Follow local radio regulations for allowed channels and transmit power.
